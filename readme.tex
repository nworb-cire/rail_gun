This readme is meant to explain where the system of differential equations implimented in the julia script came from. If the simulation is faulty, the bug may be in the derivation of the system, so it's necessary to preserve it.

The system simulates an augmented railgun. A railgun moves a projectile by passing current from one rail through the projectile into the other rail. An augmented railgun increases (or "augments") the magnetic field by an electromagnet.

The power comes from a capacitor of voltage $V_s$. The magnetic field is generated by the electromagnet ("the windings", "the inductor", "the coils", or "the wire"). Other components, such as the thyristor, are not included in the simulation, except that they contribute to $R$, the sum of all resistances. The current, $I$, passes through the projectile between the rails, and is assumed to be the same everywhere in the circuit.

The total magnetic flux in the circuit is denoted as $\phi$. The changing current in the coils and the projectile moving through magnetic field are both a source of change in flux, $\frac{d\phi}{dt}$.

The following equation can be found \href{https://physics.stackexchange.com/questions/791718/how-to-calculate-impedance-in-a-wire-moving-through-a-magnetic-field}{here}.

\[V_s-V_b=IR\]

However, the above link says $V_b$ depends on the velocity of the projectile, B-field, and projectile length in the $y$ direction. In reality, $V_b$ changes with any change in magnetic flux in the circuit. For this line of reasoning we will use two sources for change in flux (from the changing magnetic field, and from the changing size of the circuit). Read more \href{https://www.maxwells-equations.com/faraday/faradays-law.php}{here}

\[V_b=\frac{d\phi}{dt}\]
\[\phi=\int\int Bdydx\]
\[\frac{d\phi}{dt}=\int\int_0^X \frac{dB}{dt}dxdy\]

We shall soon see that the origin in the $y$ direction is arbitrary, so bounds on the integration in the $y$ direction aren't given. The $y$ direction is perpendicular to the rails and the magnetic field and $Y$ is the length of the coils in the $y$ direction. The $x$ direction is parallel to the rails and $X$ is the length of the rails. For the sake of simplicity, we will assume the H field does not vary in the $y$ direction, but the B field does because $\mu$ varies in $x$ and $y$.


the $H$ field (and by extension the $B$ field) is generated by current in the coils. \href{https://pressbooks.online.ucf.edu/osuniversityphysics2/chapter/magnetic-field-due-to-a-thin-straight-wire/}{This} link gives the following equation, where $r$ is the distance between the wire and the center of the rails in the $y$ direction, and $l$ is the signed distance in the $x$ direction between the point of interest and the current carrying segment of wire.

\[B(x) = \frac{\mu I}{4\pi} \int_{wire}\frac{r}{(l^2+r^2)^{3/2}}dl\]

This equation is for one wire. We will double it for one full coil. Since the coils \emph{mostly} run parallel to the rails, we will simplify the equations by only integrating across the $x$ direction (hence doubling: once there, once back, no side to side). We also will give it a subscript $n$, because it is the $n$th of $N$ coils. Each coil will be further than the previous coil, a distance I'm calling $\Delta r$

\[B_n(x) = \frac{\mu I}{2\pi} \int_{-x}^{X-x}\frac{r + n\Delta r}{(l^2+(r+n\Delta r)^2)^{3/2}}dl\]

To avoid repetition, we substitute $A=r+n\Delta r$. In the above equation, $\mu$ varies with $x$. It is a step function, returning the permeability of air everywhere, $\mu_0$, except where the projectile is, where it returns the permeability of the projectile, $\mu_p$ (which is an input to the script).

$B_0$ is a special case, because it is the rails. We don't integrate the entire length of the rails. We only integrate up to the end of the projectile, which is at distance $d$.

\[B_0 = \frac{\mu I}{2\pi} \int_{-x}^{d-x}\frac{r}{(l^2+r^2)^{3/2}}dl\]

The total magnetic field is given by the equation:

\[B = \sum_{n=0}^{N}B_n = \frac{\mu I}{2\pi} \left(\sum_{n=1}^{N} \int_{-x}^{X-x}\frac{r + n\Delta r}{(l^2+(r+n\Delta r)^2)^{3/2}}dl + \int_{x=0}^{x=d}\frac{r}{(l^2+r^2)^{3/2}}dl\right)\]

Nothing inside the sumation varies with time, so it makes conceptual sense to seperate it out, and it avoids repetition later on. We will call this value... $M$ for "b field modifier"????

\[M_n=\int_{x=0}^{x=X}\frac{A}{(l^2+A^2)^{3/2}}dl = \left .\frac{l}{A\sqrt{A^2+l^2}} \right\rvert_{x=0}^{x=X} \]
\[M_0=\int_{x=0}^{x=d}\frac{r}{(l^2+r^2)^{3/2}}dl = \left .\frac{l}{r\sqrt{r^2+l^2}} \right\rvert_{x=0}^{x=d}\]

$l$ in this equation is the distance from the point of interest, rather than the origin. (a negative value of $l$ is behind the point of interest). But the origin of $x$ is the beginning of the rails. If the the point of interest is at some value of $x$, then the origin of $x$ axis is $-x$ in terms of $l$.

\[M_n=\left .\frac{l}{A\sqrt{A^2+l^2}} \right\rvert_{l=-x}^{l=X-x} = A^{-1}\left (\frac{X-x}{\sqrt{A^2+(X-x)^2}} + \frac{x}{\sqrt{A^2+x^2}}\right )\]
\[M_0=\left .\frac{l}{r\sqrt{r^2+l^2}} \right\rvert_{l=-x}^{l=d-x} = r^{-1}\left (\frac{d-x}{\sqrt{r^2+(d-x)^2}} + \frac{x}{\sqrt{r^2+x^2}}\right )\]
\[M = \sum_{n=0}^{N} M_n \]
\[B=\frac{\mu I}{2\pi}M\]

Almost nothing in $M$ varies with time. Only $M_0$ does. To avoid repitition, $\sum_{n=1}^{N}M_n = M_{1:N}$

\[\frac{dB}{dt} = \frac{\mu}{2\pi}\left(\frac{dI}{dt}M + \frac{dM}{dt}I\right)\]
\[\frac{dM}{dt} = \frac{dM_0}{dt}\]
\[\frac{dM_0}{dt} = \frac{r}{\sqrt{(d-x)^2+r^2}^3}\frac{dd}{dt} \]
\[\frac{d\phi}{dt} = \int\int_0^X \frac{dB}{dt}dxdy = \int\int_0^X\frac{\mu}{2\pi}\left(\frac{dI}{dt}M +\frac{dM_0}{dt}I\right)dxdy\]

Only $M$, $\frac{dM}{dt}$ and $\mu$ vary with $x$. Since $\mu$ is a step function, it is easiest to integrate everything as $\mu_0$, and afterwards add the integral of the projectile, with $\mu_d=\mu_p-\mu_0$.
This is like taking the integral from 0 to 3 of a function that is 1 everywhere, except between 1 and 2 it is 10. The usual way to think is to take the integral from 0 to 1 (which is 1), plus the integral from 1 to 2, (10), plus the integral from 2 to 3, (again 1).
Or you could instead take the integral from 0 to 3 of 1, and then take the integral from 1 to 2. This will double count some area, so we need to subtract that out, which is 10 minus 1.
The equation below follows the same logic, except in 2 dimensions, and $\mu$ is multiplied by another function.
Suppose the length of the projectile is $L$, and it's width is $w$ .

\[\frac{d\phi}{dt} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{dI}{dt}\left(\mu_0\int\int_0^X Mdxdy + \mu_d\int\int_d^{d+L}Mdxdy\right) \]
\[ + \frac{1}{2\pi}I\left(\mu_0\int\int_0^X\frac{dM_0}{dt} dxdy + \mu_d\int\int_d^{d+L}\frac{dM_0}{dt}dxdy\right) \]
\[= \frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{dI}{dt}\left(\mu_0Y\int_0^X Mdx + \mu_dw\int_d^{d+L}Mdx\right) + \frac{1}{2\pi}I\left(\mu_0Y\int_0^X \frac{dM_0}{dt}dx + \mu_dw\int_d^{d+L}\frac{dM_0}{dt}dx\right) \]

This integration works because we set the bounds such that $\mu$ was no longer a step function in the $y$ direction, and nothing else varies in the $y$ direction.
The integral of a constant is just the width times that constant.

We now find the indefinite integral of $\frac{dM_0}{dt}$ and $M$ to substitute into the above.

\[\int M_0dx = r^{-1}\left(\int \frac{d-x}{\sqrt{r^2+(d-x)^2}}dx+\int\frac{x}{\sqrt{r^2+x^2}}dx\right)\]
\[= r^{-1}\left(-\sqrt{r^2+(d-x)^2} + \sqrt{r^2+x^2} \right) + k_0 \]
\[\int M_ndx = A^{-1}\left(\int \frac{X-x}{\sqrt{A^2+(X-x)^2}}dx + \int \frac{x}{\sqrt{A^2+x^2}}dx\right) \]
\[= A^{-1}\left(-\sqrt{A^2+(X-x)^2} + \sqrt{A^2+x^2} \right) + k_1 \]
\[\int \frac{dM_0}{dt}dx = \frac{x-d}{r\sqrt{(d-x)^2+r^2}}\frac{dd}{dt} + k_2 \]

Now that we have indefinite integral for $\int \frac{dM}{dt}dx$ and $M$ we can evaluate it on the two different bounds.

\[\int_d^{d+L}\frac{dM_0}{dt}dx = \left[\frac{x-d}{r\sqrt{(d-x)^2+r^2}}\frac{dd}{dt}\right]_d^{d+L} = \frac{L}{r\sqrt{L^2+r^2}}\frac{dd}{dt}\]

\[\int_0^X\frac{dM_0}{dt}dx = \left[\frac{x-d}{r\sqrt{(d-x)^2+r^2}}\frac{dd}{dt}\right]_0^X = r^{-1}\frac{dd}{dt}\left(\frac{X-d}{\sqrt{(d-X)^2+r^2}} + \frac{d}{\sqrt{d^2+r^2}}\right)\]

\[\int_d^{d+L}M_0dx = r^{-1}\left[-\sqrt{r^2+(d-x)^2}+\sqrt{r^2+x^2}\right]_d^{d+L} = r^{-1}\left(-\sqrt{r^2+L^2}+\sqrt{r^2+(d+L)^2} + r -\sqrt{r^2+d^2}\right)\]
\[\int_0^XM_0dx = r^{-1}\left[-\sqrt{r^2+(d-x)^2}+\sqrt{r^2+x^2}\right]_0^X = r^{-1}\left(-\sqrt{r^2+(d-X)^2}+\sqrt{r^2+X^2} - r +\sqrt{r^2+d^2}\right)\]

\[\int_d^{d+L}M_ndx = A^{-1}\left[-\sqrt{A^2+(X-x)^2}+\sqrt{A^2+x^2}\right]_d^{d+L} \]
\[ = A^{-1}\left(-\sqrt{A^2+(X-d-L)^2}+\sqrt{A^2+(d+L)^2} + \sqrt{A^2 + (X-d)^2} -\sqrt{A^2+d^2}\right)\]
\[\int_0^XM_ndx = A^{-1}\left[-\sqrt{A^2+(X-x)^2}+\sqrt{A^2+x^2}\right]_0^X = A^{-1}\left(2\sqrt{A^2+X^2}-2A\right)\]

Now, we could plug everything back in and get the mother of all equations. But why? ultimately We just want to simulate a system of differential equations. The most crucial part of this is getting a function of a vector of variables, and returning it's derivative. That vector is $[d,d',I,V_s]$, and it's derivative is $[d',d'',I',V_s']$. So any variable that is not in that output vector should be hidden away in a black box variable.
\[ D = \frac{L}{r\sqrt{L^2+r^2}} \]
\[ E = r^{-1}\left(\frac{X-d}{\sqrt{(d-X)^2+r^2}} + \frac{d}{\sqrt{d^2+r^2}}\right) \]
\[ F = r^{-1}\left(-\sqrt{r^2+L^2}+\sqrt{r^2+(d+L)^2} + r -\sqrt{r^2+d^2}\right) \]
\[ G = r^{-1}\left(-\sqrt{r^2+(d-X)^2}+\sqrt{r^2+X^2} - r +\sqrt{r^2+d^2}\right)\]
\[ H_n = A^{-1}\left(-\sqrt{A^2+(X-d-L)^2}+\sqrt{A^2+(d+L)^2} + \sqrt{A^2 + (X-d)^2} -\sqrt{A^2+d^2}\right); H = \sum_{n=1}^N H_n\]
\[ J_n = A^{-1}\left(2\sqrt{A^2+X^2}-2A\right); J = \sum_{n=1}^N J_n \]
\[ V_b = \frac{d\phi}{dt} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{dI}{dt}\left(\mu_0Y(J+G) + \mu_dw(H+F)\right) + \frac{1}{2\pi}I\left(\mu_0YE\frac{dd}{dt} + \mu_dwD\frac{dd}{dt}\right) \]

We've gone all the way down the stack and almost all the way back up again. So just as a reminder, the reason we're interested in $V_b$ is because it appears in this equation:

\[V_s-V_b=IR\]

We will use the equation for $V_b$ as it is during the computation of $V_s$. But for now we will rearange and combine with the above to solve for $\frac{dd}{dt}$ and $\frac{dI}{dt}$.

\[V_b = V_s - IR \]
\[\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{dI}{dt}\left(\mu_0Y(J+G) + \mu_dw(H+F)\right) + \frac{1}{2\pi}I\left(\mu_0YE\frac{dd}{dt} + \mu_dwD\frac{dd}{dt}\right) = V_s - IR \]
\[\frac{dI}{dt} = \frac{2\pi V_s - 2\pi IR - I\left(\mu_0YE\frac{dd}{dt} + \mu_dwD\frac{dd}{dt}\right)}{\mu_0Y(J+G) + \mu_dw(H+F)} \]
\[\frac{dd}{dt} = \frac{2\pi V_s - 2\pi IR - \frac{dI}{dt}\left(\mu_0Y(J+G) + \mu_dw(H+F)\right)}{I\left(\mu_0YE + \mu_dwD\right)} \]

Now that we have $V_b$ (which is the voltage pushing back against the supply voltage due to changing flux, faraday's law), we can talk about $V_s$, the source voltage. The source voltage is due to a capacitor. Capacitors decay exponentially to an input voltage. I.e. if we have the capacitor at 100 volts, and we're "charging" it at 80 volts, it will decay to 80 volts. You can read more \href{https://physics.stackexchange.com/questions/800396/discharge-of-a-capacitor-with-variable-backvoltage}{here}, where I got the following equation.

\[RC\frac{dV_s}{dt} + V_s = V_b \]
\[\frac{dV_s}{dt}= \frac{V_b - V_s}{RC} = \frac{-I}{C}\]

And we have one last variable to talk about, $\frac{d^2d}{dt^2}$. How the heck is the projectile going to start moving? from wikipedia article on the lorentz force we have:

\[ f = Il\times B \]

This entire time we've been thinking about things as scalars, and I sure as heck ain't gonna change that now. So we will assume the $B$ vector and $Il$ vectors are perfectly perpendicular. It should be noted that the $l$ in the equation from wikipedia is what we've been calling $w$, the width of the projectile. $B$ is of course a function of $x$, and in this case, it's when $x=d$, the distance of the projectile down the rails.

\[f = IwB(d) \]
\[f = I^2w\frac{\mu_p}{2\pi}M(d) \]

Here $f$ is the lorentz force. The total forces acting on the projectile are $f_{net}=f-f_s$ where $f_s$ is the force due to friction against the rails.

\[f_{net}=m\frac{d^2d}{dt^2} \]
\[\frac{d^2d}{dt^2} = m^{-1}\left(I^2w\frac{\mu_p}{2\pi}M(d) - f_s\right) \]

And... I think that's it. That's everything. There should now be an equation for each of the four output variables now. It's time to implement this in the code! Good luck dear reader!

OH NO! dear reader, you've continued reading on! which implies that there was indeed more to read, and we were not done creating out system of differential equations. You have come upon bad luck.

I said that we had a closed form function for each of the variables in $du$. And indeed that is correct. What we do not have is a closed form for each variable in $du$ \emph{only in terms of }$u$. And that's the problem. To be more specific, the equation for $\frac{dd}{dt}$ depends on $\frac{dI}{dt}$. Since the rest of this document only has derivatives and anti derivatives with respect to time (whereas before we also had with respect to $x$ or $l$), I will switch to using newton's notation. 

I will list each element of $du$ as a function $f$ of it's dependent variables. (For this argument it is not relevant what the function is, only it's inputs. If you'd like a refresher, scroll up.)

\[ d' = f_0(I,I', V_s) \]

This is the offending function, because of the input $I'$, which is not in $u$. You may think "We have an equation for $I'$. Why not just substitute?" Here is the equation for $I'$:

\[ I' = f_1(I, d') \]

Because $I'$ depends on $d'$ this approach will go nowhere. Here are the rest of the equations.

\[ d'' = f_2(I,d) \]
\[ V_s' = f_3(I) \]

Well, if the problem is that $I'$ is not part of $u$, why don't we just add it? We will of course, have to add it's derivative to $du$, and we don't have a formula for $I''$.

Ah, but we do! We are free to choose any of these equations and take the derivative of both sides. If we choose one dependent on $I'$, it's derivative will contain $I''$. Let us choose our problem equation.

\[ d'' = f_0'(I,I',I'',V_s,V_s') \]

Because of the chainrule, this equation has more variables in it. Now, $I''$ is an implicit function of $I, I', d''$. We call this function $g$.

\[ I'' = g(I,I',d'',V_s,V_s') \]

Hazzah! We've now fixed our problem with $d'$, because now all its dependent variables are in $u$. But the astute reader has probably noticed something else is wrong. $I''$ depends on $d''$ and $V_s'$, neither of which are in $u$. We have just pushed the problem down one level. In fact we've compounded the problem because we now have 2 variables not in $u$, whereas before we had one.

Hold up. We can substitute in other values for $d''$ and $V_s'$. So we have:

\[ I'' = g(I,I',f_2(I,d),V_s,f_3(I)) \]

And so $I''$ is some function $g$. Now to find out what that function is!

We begin by taking the derivate of $d'$.

\[ d' = \frac{2\pi V_s - 2\pi IR - I'\left(\mu_0Y(J+G) + \mu_dw(H+F)\right)}{I\left(\mu_0YE + \mu_dwD\right)} \]

Despite all the decluttering substitutions we've made, this is still a horrible mess. More substitutions!

\[ K = \mu_0Y(J+G) + \mu_dw(H+F) \]
\[ O = \mu_0YE + \mu_dwD \]
\[ d' = \frac{2\pi V_s - 2\pi IR - I'K}{IO} \]
\[ d' = \frac{2\pi V_s}{IO}-\frac{2\pi R}{O}-\frac{I'K}{IO} \]
\[ d'' = \frac{2\pi V_s'I-2\pi V_sI' - KI''I + KI'^2}{OI^2} \]

We solve for $I''$.

\[ I'' = \frac{d''OI^2 - 2\pi V_s'I + 2\pi V_sI' -KI'^2}{-KI} \]

And substitute in the values of $V_s'$ and $d''$

\[ I'' = \frac{m^{-1}\left(I^2w\frac{\mu_p}{2\pi}M(d) - f_s\right)OI^2 - 2\pi \frac{-I}{C}I + 2\pi V_sI' -KI'^2}{-KI} \]

And now to implement this in code. Good luck dear reader!
